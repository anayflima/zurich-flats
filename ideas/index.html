A company of companies: we build products inside, AI helps run them. Updated 15 Nov 2026

The idea in one line

A venture-funded company of companies. We build several products inside one company, each aiming for millions (then tens of millions) in ARR, all running on one shared AI company machine for GTM, onboarding, support and ops. We keep the companies and we do not sell the machine.

Why now

  • Big, horizontal TAMs are exactly where the AI labs go next (a lab already shipped a Granola-type notes app). Focused markets are below their radar.
  • AI made building cheap. The bottleneck is now running a company: distribution, onboarding, support, ops. That is what the machine does.
  • We are funded, so we can run several bets in parallel instead of one at a time, hire a platform team early and buy companies sooner.

Proof that it can work

  • Rippling builds many products in parallel on one data layer: about $570M ARR (2025).
  • Bending Spoons runs many brands on one shared stack: about $1.31B revenue (2025).
  • Zoho runs 55+ apps on one platform: about $1.4B revenue.
  • Investors already back the model: Thrive Holdings ($3B+), General Catalyst Creation ($1.5B).

Company of companies Lead idea

What it is

One company that builds and owns several products. Each product has its own brand and a small pod (2 to 3 people). A central team builds the company machine: the shared AI system that launches and runs every product. Humans do the 0 to 1 (pick the market, talk to customers, shape the product); the machine does the repeatable work. When a product shows product-market fit, it gets more people and money. Later, with cash flow, we also buy stagnant software companies and move them onto the machine (the Bending Spoons step).

How it differs from what exists

ModelWhat they doOur difference

Pros

  • Many shots on goal; we learn fast what works.
  • Funding lets us run several pods at once and hire a strong platform team early.
  • The machine is a compounding asset and a real moat.
  • Focused markets avoid the labs and big incumbents.
  • Fits us: ML, finance and PE exposure, and strong engineering and product.
  • Investors already fund this shape (Rippling, Bending Spoons, Thrive Holdings).
  • Later M&A engine: stagnant software sells for about 3.5 to 5.5x ARR.

Cons and risks

  • Each product still needs its own PMF and its own channel.
  • Focus can split; lempire and Zoho grew one hit first, then added products.
  • The story to investors must add up to a venture outcome, not a set of lifestyle businesses.
  • Burn: several pods plus a platform team spend fast if the kill rules are soft.
  • Running many brands, legal entities and support queues adds overhead.
  • AI makes building cheap for everyone, so focused markets fill up faster too.

Open questions

  • Same buyer (cross-sell, like Rippling or lempire) or unrelated markets (portfolio, like Bending Spoons)?
  • One brand or many brands under one parent?
  • How many pods at once: 3, 5 or 8?
  • When does M&A start: from day one, or only after the first products hit about $1M ARR?
  • How do we report it to investors: total ARR, cash flow, or per-product metrics?

Playbook (draft)

Idea filter: a market passes when

  • B2B buyers who already pay for software or services in this area.
  • A channel we can reach: a community, a marketplace, SEO, a clear outbound list.
  • Too focused for the labs to care, but big enough for $5M to $20M ARR.
  • Testable in 4 to 6 weeks with the machine.
  • Ideally fits our backgrounds or shares a buyer with another product.

Stages and gates

  1. Explore (2 to 4 weeks): 20+ customer conversations, a landing page from the machine, a waitlist or letters of intent.
  2. Build (4 to 6 weeks): MVP on shared auth, billing and onboarding; first paying users.
  3. Prove (about 90 days): kill if there are no paying customers or no MRR growth; keep the learnings in the machine.
  4. Scale: steady monthly MRR growth and good retention earn a bigger pod and budget.
  5. Run: the machine runs GTM, support and ops; humans focus on the next product.

The company machine (shared pieces)

  • GTM machine: landing page, LinkedIn page, daily posts, outbound, ads.
  • Auto onboarding and product tours.
  • Self-healing support and ops agents.
  • Shared auth, billing, analytics and one dashboard across products.
  • A launch template, so every new product starts from the same base.

Capital rules (illustrative, our assumptions)

  • Fixed budget per pod per stage; money follows the gates, not opinions.
  • Example at about $10M a year of spend: platform and machine team about $3 to 4M, 6 to 8 pods at about $0.5 to 0.8M each.
  • Buying companies later uses a hurdle rate, like Constellation (about 20 to 25% IRR).

These are planning assumptions, not researched numbers.

AWS for agent automations Parked

The idea

Cloud infrastructure that runs AI agents and automations for you, so nobody needs a Mac mini running OpenClaw 24/7. Set up every day do X, Y, Z online; it runs on our servers and results come to you.

Already exists

  • Hosted OpenClaw: OpenClaw Cloud, V2 Cloud, HoopyClaw, or a cheap VPS.
  • Developer infra: Modal, E2B, Trigger.dev.
  • No-code agents: Lindy, Zapier Agents, Manus, n8n Cloud, Relevance AI.
  • Big labs: ChatGPT Scheduled Tasks and Agent Mode, Claude Managed Agents.

Pros

  • Real pain and recurring revenue.
  • Fits our ML and infra background.
  • Growing market; can start narrow.

Cons

  • Big labs are already there.
  • Crowded and generic without a wedge.
  • Capital and ops heavy; thin margins.
  • Trust and security of logins on our servers.

Verdict

Not a standalone product for now. Keep it as the internal infrastructure of the company machine.

The Company Company Reference

What they do (from thecompany.company)

  • Tagline: The AI that runs your business. A team plus a company of always-on agents, with shared memory and all your tools, working 24/7.
  • The company is shown as departments (Engineering, Product and Design, Go-to-Market, Operations) with named agents working next to humans.
  • Agents take tasks like reading 212 replies to a welcome email, writing an onboarding spec, closing the books, refunding a double charge, shipping a checkout; humans review and approve.
  • Sold as SaaS: Individual and Teams plans (Pro, Max, Ultra).

Why it matters to us

  • Validation: a funded team bets that agents can run every department.
  • They sell the shovels; we mine. They make money from the platform, we make money from the companies the platform runs.
  • We could use tools like this inside our machine instead of building every piece.
  • Risk: if our machine gets very good, selling it is tempting, and that puts us back in their market.

Big-TAM ideas are exactly what the labs go after (a lab shipped something that replaces a Granola-type app). Maybe pick smaller TAMs that are not attractive enough for them.

14 Nov, 3:27: the Bending Spoons idea

Bending Spoons buys companies that stopped growing, keeps the users, rebuilds with far fewer people and raises revenue. Our version: a company factory used internally, not sold. We do the 0 to 1, then AI runs GTM, users and sales. Products can have different names but belong to one company.

14 Nov, 1:55: how it would work

Develop several ideas in parallel and make everything reproducible: a GTM machine, self-healing systems, auto LinkedIn page and daily posts, quick onboarding. Put human time where PMF shows up. Later, with money, buy companies and make them efficient.

14 Nov, late: The Company Company

They sell this layer to companies to make them more efficient. We would build it internally to launch our own companies and make money from them.

15 Nov: funded, not bootstrapped

Many companies make millions in ARR; several of them together can add up to a lot. We are venture-funded, so this is not bootstrapping: a big company of companies, with the products built inside, scaled with one playbook.
